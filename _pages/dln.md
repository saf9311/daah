Gemini's Response:
Digital Literacy Beyond the Surface: An Interactive Media Perspective
At twenty, my generation has spent a decade being labeled "digital natives," a condescending shorthand that confuses thumb agility with critical consciousness. Knowing how to hotkey a timeline in Premiere, spin up a quick Discord server, or prompt an LLM to debug a rogue array doesn’t mean we understand digital literacy. Sitting in a multidisciplinary seminar—surrounded by a Computer Science major debugging C++ memory leaks, an Environmental Studies major tracking geospatial soil-moisture datasets, and a Creative Writing major interrogating narrative voice—has completely destabilized what I thought my medium was. Interactive Media isn’t just about making things look reactive or shiny; it’s about negotiating power, memory, and code in a networked world.
Daily Workflows, Content Creation, and Social Architecture
My academic and creative rhythm relies on a stack built for friction and fluidity rather than siloed productivity:
Capture & Synthesis: Zotero paired with Obsidian for literature mapping, treating research notes as a bidirectional graph rather than a linear bibliography.
Prototyping & Spatial Thought: Figma for interface choreography, TouchDesigner or p5.js for generative/live behavior, and Notion as a communal project hub.
Asset & Version Hygiene: Git with Git-LFS coupled with cloud staging buckets, treating media assets (textures, audio stems, vector SVGs) with the same provenance discipline as source code.
Social/Discursive Layer: Asynchronous micro-communities via private Discord pods or GitHub discussion boards where critique happens in-situ through pull requests, video annotations, and shared mood boards.
Social interaction online isn't passive consumption anymore; it’s performance architecture. When I curate a shared project repository or build an interactive web-toy, I’m designing an invitation for someone else to break, fork, or inhabit my logic.
Data Management and Manipulation: From Archives to Materials
As an Interactive Media major, data isn't a spreadsheet locked in a vault; it’s raw tactile material. For research data management, I lean on relational structures (Airtable or lightweight SQLite databases) tagged with rich JSON metadata schemas capturing provenance, license restrictions, and capture conditions. When dealing with unstructured cultural artifacts—say, oral history audio transcripts or archival film stills—manipulation means writing custom Python scripts via Pandas and BeautifulSoup to clean, tokenize, and chunk text, or processing video feeds through OpenCV for optical flow analysis and frame-diff telemetry.
Crucially, digital literacy here means recognizing that data cleaning is an interpretive act. Deciding which NaN values to drop, how to normalize sentiment scores, or how to bucket geographic coordinates is editorial storytelling disguised as math. Every transformation alters the ontological weight of the archive.
The Interdisciplinary Crucible and Computational Thinking
The real education happens in the cross-pollination of the studio critique room.
When a Creative Writing major tears apart a linear narrative structure because "space can breathe time differently," it forces my Interactive Media brain to rethink state machines not as rigid flowchart logic, but as branching emotional geographies and non-linear affective loops.
When an Engineering/CS student breaks down algorithmic time complexity (scaling down heavy loops or implementing spatial hashing), it grounds my real-time generative art installations in hardware and frame-rate reality.
Collaboration forces computational thinking to evolve from mechanical problem-solving ("how do I loop this array?") into systemic empathy ("what feedback loops does this interface trigger in the user’s cognitive load, attention economy, and privacy footprint?").
New Horizons: Digital Humanities, Cultural Data, and Adversarial AI
Digital Humanities (DH) shifts the horizon from reading about culture to operationalizing cultural critique. Mapping 19th-century shipping manifests or spatializing modernist poetry reveals structural macro-trends invisible to close reading.
In the shadow of generative AI, digital literacy demands moving past prompt-consumerism into adversarial and material engagement. Instead of asking an LLM to write boilerplate code or generate generic asset textures, I’m learning to look at AI models as lossy cultural compresses. How do we train a small, local sentiment classifier on a localized, non-hegemonic corpus? How do we visualize training drift, latent space collapse, or dataset provenance? DH teaches us that computation is archival archaeology: we are excavating the biases baked into the substrate of digital tools.
Critical Self-Assessment and Where Next
Honestly? My technical confidence outpaces my ethical audit framework. I can wire together a real-time face-tracking particle system or deploy a web-scraping pipeline in an afternoon, but I often rush past metadata lineage or accessibility implications (screen reader semantic trees, color contrast edge cases, low-bandwidth footprint).
Moving forward, I want to trade superficial interactivity for ecological and somatic computing—exploring how biometric or spatial inputs can map environmental precarity rather than corporate engagement metrics. Digital literacy at twenty isn't mastery; it's realizing every interface is a political choice, and learning how to author resilient alternatives.

Claude's Response:
Reflection on Digital Literacy
As a 20-year-old Interactive Media major, I used to assume digital literacy was something I had already finished learning. I grew up editing photos on my phone and posting to Instagram and TikTok. This course has shown me that fluency with tools is only the surface. Digital literacy is about understanding how digital information is made, moved, and interpreted, and questioning the systems that shape what I see.
Content creation and social interaction are where I am strongest. I use Photoshop, Illustrator, and Premiere Pro to build images and video, and Figma to prototype interfaces. Working in these programs has taught me that every choice, from color contrast to caption placement to alt text, affects who can access a piece and how they will read it. Socially, I treat each platform as its own audience: a TikTok, an Instagram carousel, and a Discord post all call for different tones and formats. I also credit other creators and consider how a post might travel beyond its intended audience.
My academic workflow is organized but not advanced. I search Google Scholar and my library databases, store citations in Zotero, share files through Google Drive, and track deadlines in Notion. For data, I mostly use Excel or Google Sheets to sort, filter, and chart. I have learned that research is a digital skill in itself. Finding a source quickly matters less than asking who produced it, how the data was collected, and what might be missing.
This is where my gaps show. I get nervous with large datasets, complex formulas, and any programming beyond the HTML, CSS, and JavaScript I use in interactive projects. I tend to treat data as a separate world from design, even though the most compelling interactive work is often driven by data. I want to become comfortable with tools like Python, Tableau, or Observable so I can clean, analyze, and visualize information rather than only decorating it.
That is why an interdisciplinary room is so valuable. A Computer Science student may naturally see a problem as a series of functions. A Film and New Media student may think first about sequence and audience. An Environmental Studies student may already work with mapping tools and climate datasets, and an Engineering student may focus on constraints and testing. A Literature or Creative Writing student may be trained to read for tone and hidden assumptions. Collaborating shows me that computational thinking, which means breaking a problem into parts, spotting patterns, and testing solutions, is not limited to code. I already do it when I storyboard an interface or user-test a prototype, while a programming partner might show me how to script a repetitive task and I can help make their work visible and usable.
Digital humanities has changed how I think about my own field. I used to connect technology with design and entertainment. Now I see that text analysis can reveal patterns across thousands of novels, digital archives can preserve fragile histories, and maps can show how stories are tied to places. As an Interactive Media student, I am excited by the chance to build interfaces that let people explore these materials themselves, such as a timeline of an oral-history archive or an interactive map of a poem's settings. That would make scholarship more open, but it also means asking whose stories are included and who decided how they were categorized.
AI is the area I most want to understand. I have used generative tools for brainstorming and moodboards, but I have also seen them produce confident errors, invented sources, and images that repeat stereotypes. I treat AI as a starting point, never a final answer, and I verify anything factual. What I still lack is a real sense of how these systems are trained, where their biases come from, and how to think about credit and consent for the creators whose work fed them. I want to learn enough about datasets and machine learning to critique AI, not only use it.
The biggest lesson of this course is that digital literacy is not a fixed skill I either have or lack. It is a habit of curiosity and healthy doubt that must keep developing as technology does. My strengths are visual communication, content creation, and understanding audiences. My next steps are data analysis, computational thinking, and a critical understanding of AI. I want to become someone who does not just operate technology but understands how it works, questions its effects, and uses it creatively to help others see the world more clearly.
