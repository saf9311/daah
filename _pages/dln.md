My Response:
Reflection on Digital Literacy
As a 17-year-old university student majoring in Interactive Media, I am already very used to using digital technology, so I sometimes assume that I am already digitally literate. I use my phone, social media, editing software, online research tools, and different digital platforms every day. However, this course has made me realize that digital literacy is much more than simply knowing how to use technology. It also involves understanding how digital information is created, shared, analyzed, and interpreted, and being able to think critically about the technologies that shape the way we see the world.
In my daily and academic life, I already use a variety of digital strategies for content creation and communication. For example, I use platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, and other social media to interact with people and share visual content. As an Interactive Media student, I also work with tools such as Adobe Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere Pro, and other creative software to edit images, videos, and designs. These tools allow me to communicate ideas visually instead of relying only on written text. I have also learned that creating digital content involves making decisions about audience, composition, accessibility, and the way information is presented.
For academic work, I regularly rely on online databases, Google, digital libraries, and other research resources to find information. I use tools such as Google Drive and Microsoft Office to organize assignments and collaborate with others. I am becoming more aware that research itself is a digital skill. It is not enough to find information quickly; I need to evaluate whether a source is reliable, understand where its information comes from, and recognize possible bias. Managing files, organizing research, keeping track of sources, and presenting information clearly are all important parts of digital literacy.
One of the most interesting aspects of having an interdisciplinary class is realizing that everyone approaches technology differently. Someone studying Computer Science may be comfortable with coding and computational systems, while someone studying Film and New Media might have more experience with visual storytelling and editing. A student studying Environmental Studies might work with environmental datasets and mapping tools, while someone studying Literature or Creative Writing might be more experienced with analyzing language, narratives, and digital texts. As an Interactive Media student, I bring a different perspective because I am interested in how technology, design, communication, and human interaction come together.
This difference in skills can actually make collaboration more valuable. Working with people from other disciplines can expose me to methods that I would not normally use. For example, someone with programming experience might approach a creative problem by breaking it into smaller steps, while I might approach the same problem through design and user experience. Combining these approaches can help develop computational thinking. Computational thinking is not only about coding; it is also about recognizing patterns, breaking complicated problems into manageable parts, testing solutions, and thinking systematically.
Digital humanities is another area that has expanded my understanding of what technology can do. Before learning about it, I mostly connected digital technology with design, media, and entertainment. Digital humanities shows how computational tools can also be used to study history, literature, culture, language, and society. Text analysis, digital archives, data visualization, mapping, and interactive storytelling can create completely new ways of studying subjects that traditionally depended on books and written analysis. This makes me interested in how Interactive Media could be used to make research more engaging and accessible.
At the same time, I recognize that I still have areas where I need to improve. I am comfortable with many creative and social platforms, but I do not always feel confident when working with large datasets, spreadsheets, programming, or more advanced data analysis. I understand the basic idea of manipulating information, but I would like to become more comfortable using data to support creative projects rather than treating it as something separate from design. I also want to develop a better understanding of how AI systems work and how they can be used responsibly.
AI is especially interesting because it is already changing creative and academic work. I want to explore how AI can support brainstorming, research, design, data analysis, and content creation without replacing critical thinking or creativity. I think an important part of digital literacy today is knowing when to use AI, how to question its results, and how to recognize that AI-generated information can contain errors or biases.
Overall, this course has helped me understand that digital literacy is not a fixed skill that I either have or do not have. It is something that continues to develop as technology changes. I already have strengths in visual communication, digital content creation, and online interaction, but I want to expand those skills into areas such as data analysis, computational thinking, digital humanities, and AI. Most importantly, I want to become someone who does not simply use technology, but understands how it works, questions its impact, and finds creative ways to use it to understand and communicate ideas.
